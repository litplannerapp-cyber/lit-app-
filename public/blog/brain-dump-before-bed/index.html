How to do a brain dump before bed (and actually fall asleep)

You turn off the light, and your head turns on. Tomorrow's meeting, the email you forgot, the gift you still need to buy. Here is a five-minute routine to get all of it out before you sleep.

24 November 2026 · 5 min read

What a brain dump is

A brain dump is exactly what it sounds like: you take everything that is circling in your head and put it somewhere outside it. No order, no categories, no judgement. Tasks, worries, ideas, half-remembered errands, all in one place.

It is not journaling and it is not planning. You are not trying to understand your day or organise your week. You are just emptying the room so you can turn off the light.

Why it helps you fall asleep

Your brain is very good at reminding you of unfinished things, and it likes to do it at night, when nothing else is competing for attention. Once those things are written down somewhere you trust, there is less reason for your mind to keep rehearsing them.

There is research behind this too. In a 2018 sleep-lab study at Baylor University, 57 young adults spent five minutes before bed writing either a to-do list for the coming days or a list of things they had already done. The to-do list group fell asleep about nine minutes faster on average, and the more specific their list, the faster they fell asleep (Scullin et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology: General).

Write what is still waiting for you, not what you already did. Specific beats vague.

The five-minute brain dump

  1. Pick one place and always use the same one. A notebook on the bedside table or one app. If your thoughts can land in five different places, your brain will not trust any of them.
  2. Set a timer for five minutes. Short enough that you will actually do it every night, long enough to catch the stragglers.
  3. Write everything, as it comes. "Reply to Anna about Saturday." "Book the dentist." "Is the boiler making that noise again?" One line each. Do not sort, do not fix, do not open your email.
  4. Make tasks specific. "Christmas" is a worry. "Order the lights for the shelf" is a task. The study above found specific lists worked best, so push each line one step closer to an action.
  5. Choose tomorrow's three. Look at the list and pick only three things that would make tomorrow feel good if they got done. Everything else can wait in the list. It is safe there.
  6. Close it and put the phone away. The dump is done. Anything new that pops up gets one line, then lights out.

What not to do

  • Do not start solving. The moment you open the email to "just quickly reply", you are working, not winding down.
  • Do not turn it into a diary of the day. Listing what you already did felt calming in theory, but in the study it was the slower route to sleep.
  • Do not aim for a perfect list. Messy is fine. The goal is an empty head, not a beautiful page.

Make it a ritual, not a chore

It works best when it is the same few minutes every night: tea, lamp on, list, lamp off. After a week it stops feeling like a task and starts feeling like closing the day. Many people find the Sunday version even more useful: one longer dump on Sunday evening, so Monday morning starts with a clear head instead of a full one.
